Selling a House in an HOA Community in DFW

Published November 20, 2026 · Dallas-Fort Worth, Texas
Back to All Articles

If you're selling a home in a Dallas-Fort Worth HOA community, you're dealing with an extra layer of complexity beyond the typical home sale. From transfer fees and architectural approvals to outstanding dues and restrictive covenants, HOA regulations can significantly impact your timeline and bottom line. Understanding Texas HOA laws and your obligations as a seller will help you navigate this process smoothly and avoid costly surprises at closing.

Understanding HOA Requirements When Selling in Texas

Texas has specific laws governing homeowners associations that directly affect your sale. Under the Texas Property Code, sellers in HOA communities must provide buyers with certain documentation before closing, including the HOA's governing documents, financial statements, and a resale certificate.

The resale certificate is particularly important. This document, which typically costs between $200-$500 in DFW communities, details your account status, any outstanding fees, upcoming assessments, and the HOA's financial health. Texas law requires HOAs to provide this certificate within 10 business days of your request, though many charge expedite fees for faster service.

Missing these disclosure requirements can delay your closing or even give buyers legal grounds to back out of the contract. In Texas's competitive real estate market, any delay can mean the difference between a successful sale and a lost opportunity.

Common HOA Issues That Can Derail Your Sale

Outstanding Dues and Assessments

One of the most common obstacles sellers face is outstanding HOA dues. In Texas, HOAs have significant collection powers, including the ability to place liens on your property. These liens must be satisfied before you can transfer clear title to a buyer.

Even if you're current on regular dues, special assessments approved by the HOA board can create unexpected costs at closing. Many DFW communities have recently passed assessments for road repairs, pool renovations, or gate system upgrades that can range from a few hundred to several thousand dollars per homeowner.

Architectural Violations and Compliance Issues

Your HOA's architectural control committee likely has jurisdiction over everything from paint colors to fence heights. Existing violations must typically be resolved before closing, which can be problematic if you've been living with that unapproved storage shed or fence extension for years.

In Texas HOA communities, common compliance issues include:

  • Exterior paint colors not matching approved palettes
  • Unapproved landscaping or dead/dying plants
  • Vehicles parked in driveways overnight (common in Frisco and Plano communities)
  • Basketball goals or play equipment in front yards
  • Visible trash cans outside designated days
  • Roof damage or discoloration requiring replacement

Addressing these violations before listing can prevent delays, but sometimes the cost and time required make a traditional sale impractical.

Rental Restrictions and Transfer Limitations

Many newer DFW communities, particularly in Prosper, Celina, and parts of Frisco, have rental restrictions that limit the percentage of homes that can be rented at any given time. If your community has reached its rental cap, buyers planning to use the property as an investment may not be eligible to purchase, significantly reducing your buyer pool.

Some HOAs also enforce transfer fees, right of first refusal clauses, or approval processes for potential buyers. While Texas law limits some of these practices, they're still common enough to complicate sales.

The Financial Impact of HOA Rules on Your Sale

Beyond compliance costs, HOA communities come with ongoing financial obligations that affect your net proceeds. Monthly or annual HOA dues in DFW typically range from $400 to over $3,000 annually, depending on amenities. Luxury communities in Southlake, Colleyville, and parts of McKinney often have premium dues that can be dealbreakers for some buyers.

At closing, you'll be responsible for prorating HOA dues and paying any transfer or document fees required by your association. These costs can add up quickly:

  • Resale certificate: $200-$500
  • Transfer fee: $150-$400 (some communities)
  • Document preparation fees: $50-$200
  • Outstanding violations or fines: varies widely
  • Prorated dues through closing

If your HOA requires property improvements before sale, you could face thousands more in costs for painting, landscaping, roof repairs, or other compliance issues.

Texas-Specific HOA Laws That Protect Sellers

Texas does offer some protections for homeowners selling in HOA communities. Under Texas Property Code Section 209, HOAs must follow specific procedures before foreclosing on liens, and they can only foreclose for unpaid assessments exceeding a certain threshold or age.

Additionally, Texas law limits an HOA's ability to unreasonably withhold approval of a buyer. If your HOA has buyer approval requirements, they must act within a reasonable timeframe and cannot arbitrarily reject qualified buyers.

However, these protections don't eliminate the fundamental challenges of selling in an HOA community. Even with legal safeguards, the documentation requirements, potential for violations, and financial obligations remain significant considerations.

Strategies for a Smooth HOA Sale

Start Early With Documentation

Request your resale certificate and HOA documents as soon as you decide to sell. The 10-day requirement means you could face delays if you wait until you have a buyer. Having documents ready shows buyers you're serious and professional.

Address Violations Proactively

Walk your property with a copy of your HOA's architectural guidelines and compliance rules. Address obvious violations before listing if you're pursuing a traditional sale. If violations are extensive or expensive, consider whether a cash sale might net you more after accounting for repair costs.

Understand Your Buyer's Perspective

High HOA dues can be a significant obstacle, particularly in Texas where property taxes are already substantial (since Texas has no state income tax, property taxes tend to be higher than in many other states). Be prepared to justify the value of HOA amenities and services when marketing your home.

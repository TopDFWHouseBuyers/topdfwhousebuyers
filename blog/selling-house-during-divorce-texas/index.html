Understanding Texas Community Property Laws

Texas is one of nine community property states, which significantly impacts how your home is divided during divorce. Any property acquired during your marriage is generally considered community property, meaning both spouses have equal ownership regardless of whose name is on the title.

This community property framework applies to:

  • The marital home purchased during the marriage
  • Equity built up in the home during the marriage
  • Improvements or renovations made with marital funds
  • The mortgage debt on the property

However, if one spouse owned the home before marriage or inherited it, that portion may be considered separate property. The equity gained during the marriage would still be community property, making valuations more complex.

Texas Homestead Exemption Considerations

Texas offers generous homestead exemptions that protect your primary residence from most creditors. During divorce, the court may award temporary exclusive use of the homestead to one spouse, especially when children are involved. Understanding how this affects your timeline for selling is crucial when planning your next steps.

Your Options for the Family Home During Divorce

When divorcing in the DFW area, you typically have three main options for handling your shared property:

Option 1: Sell the House and Split the Proceeds

This is often the cleanest solution, allowing both parties to move forward independently. Selling provides:

  • Equal division of equity without ongoing disputes
  • A fresh financial start for both parties
  • No continued joint liability on the mortgage
  • Freedom from shared property maintenance responsibilities

In the current DFW market, selling can be particularly advantageous. The Dallas-Fort Worth area continues to see strong demand, with cities like Frisco, McKinney, and Southlake attracting buyers seeking quality schools and amenities.

Option 2: One Spouse Buys Out the Other

If one spouse wants to keep the family home—perhaps to maintain stability for children—a buyout may work. This requires:

  • Refinancing the mortgage in one spouse's name only
  • Qualifying for the loan based on individual income
  • Having funds available to buy out the other's equity share
  • Agreement on the home's current market value

In expensive DFW submarkets like Colleyville, Keller, or Grapevine, qualifying for a refinance on a single income can be challenging, making this option less feasible for many divorcing couples.

Option 3: Co-own Temporarily

Some couples agree to delay selling, especially if the market is down or children need stability. However, this option comes with risks:

  • Both remain liable for mortgage payments
  • Disagreements over maintenance and repairs may arise
  • One spouse's credit could be damaged if the other misses payments
  • Emotional closure becomes more difficult

The Real Costs of Selling During Divorce in Texas

Traditional home sales in the DFW area come with significant expenses that reduce your net proceeds:

  • Real estate agent commissions: Typically 6% of the sale price (3% for listing agent, 3% for buyer's agent)
  • Closing costs: Usually 1-3% of the sale price, including title insurance, transfer taxes, and attorney fees
  • Repairs and updates: Buyers in competitive DFW markets expect move-in ready homes
  • Staging and photography: Necessary to compete in areas like Richardson, Allen, or Prosper
  • Carrying costs: Mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and HOA fees during the listing period

Many HOAs in planned communities throughout Carrollton, The Colony, and Flower Mound have strict exterior maintenance requirements that must be met before selling. Factor these costs into your budget.

Texas has no state income tax, which means you won't pay state taxes on any capital gains from your home sale—a significant advantage. However, you'll still need to consider federal capital gains taxes if your profit exceeds exemption thresholds.

Navigating the Legal Process in Texas

Texas divorce law requires specific steps when selling marital property:

Court Approval May Be Required

Depending on where you are in the divorce process, you may need court approval before selling. If a temporary restraining order or injunction is in place, both spouses typically cannot sell, transfer, or encumber property without agreement or court permission.

Both Spouses Must Sign

Even if only one spouse is on the title, Texas community property laws generally require both spouses to sign the deed when selling the marital home. This protects both parties' interests in the property.

Proceed Division Should Be Specified

Your divorce decree or mediated settlement agreement should clearly state how sale proceeds will be divided. This prevents disputes at closing and ensures the title company can properly disburse funds.

Consider Tax Implications

The IRS allows married couples filing jointly to exclude up to $500,000 in capital gains from the sale of a primary residence ($250,000 for individuals). Timing your sale relative to your divorce finalization could impact your tax liability, so consult with a tax professional familiar with Texas real estate.

Special Considerations for DFW Homeowners

The Dallas-Fort Worth market has unique characteristics that affect divorce home sales:

Strong Market Conditions

The DFW metroplex continues to experience population growth and economic expansion. Cities like Celina and Prosper are among the fastest-growing in the nation, creating strong buyer demand. This generally favorable market can work in your favor when selling.

Property Tax Considerations

Texas has relatively high property taxes compared to other states. When divorcing, consider how property tax obligations will be handled up until closing. Prorated taxes are typically addressed at closing, but be clear about who's responsible for any supplemental assessments.

HOA Rules and Restrictions

Many DFW homes, particularly in newer communities throughout Frisco, Lewisville, Coppell, and surrounding areas, are governed by homeowners associations. Review your HOA's governing documents for any restrictions on sales, required disclosures, or transfer fees that could affect your transaction.

Avoiding Foreclosure

If mortgage payments become difficult during divorce proceedings, act quickly. Texas follows a non-judicial foreclosure process that can move relatively fast compared to other states. The typical timeline from first missed payment to foreclosure sale is 5-6 months, though this varies. Selling before foreclosure protects both spouses' credit and maximizes equity recovery.

Steps to Sell Your House During Divorce in Texas

Follow these steps to navigate the process smoothly:

  1. Communicate with your spouse: Even if the relationship is strained, try to agree on the basic approach to selling
  2. Determine your home's value: Get a professional appraisal or comparative market analysis for homes in your Arlington, North Richland Hills, Euless, Hurst, Bedford, or other DFW neighborhood
  3. Consult with your divorce attorney: Ensure you understand any legal restrictions and get proper authorization to proceed
  4. Decide on selling method: Traditional listing vs. cash sale based on your timeline and circumstances
  5. Clear title issues: Resolve any liens, judgments, or other title problems early in the process
  6. Agree on proceed division: Document in writing how funds will be split to prevent closing delays
  7. Coordinate with your divorce timeline: Align the closing date with your divorce proceedings when possible
  8. Complete required disclosures: Texas law requires specific disclosures about property condition

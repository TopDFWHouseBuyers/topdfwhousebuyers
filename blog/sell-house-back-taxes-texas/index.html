How to Sell a House With Back Taxes in Texas

Published November 16, 2026 · Dallas-Fort Worth, Texas
Back to All Articles

Falling behind on property taxes in Texas can feel overwhelming, especially when you're facing potential foreclosure. If you own a home in the Dallas-Fort Worth area and have unpaid property taxes, you're not alone—and you do have options. Understanding Texas-specific tax laws and your choices for selling can help you move forward and protect your financial future.

Understanding Property Tax Delinquency in Texas

Texas relies heavily on property taxes to fund local schools, roads, and services—there's no state income tax here, which means property tax rates can be significant. In the DFW area, combined tax rates typically range from 2% to 3% of your home's assessed value annually. When you fall behind on these payments, the consequences escalate quickly.

Once your property taxes become delinquent (unpaid by February 1st of the year following the tax year), penalties and interest begin accumulating immediately. Texas law allows for a 6% penalty in the first month, additional penalties for each subsequent month, and interest charges of up to 12% annually. These costs add up fast, turning a manageable debt into a substantial burden.

The Texas Tax Foreclosure Timeline

In Texas, taxing authorities can file a tax lien on your property as soon as taxes become delinquent. While they typically wait to pursue foreclosure, they have the legal right to do so as early as July 1st of the year your taxes become delinquent. Most counties in the DFW area, including Dallas, Tarrant, Collin, and Denton counties, will send multiple notices before initiating foreclosure proceedings.

The foreclosure process for tax-delinquent properties in Texas moves through the court system and can take anywhere from several months to a few years, depending on the county's backlog. However, once a judgment is entered against your property, it can be sold at a tax sale on the first Tuesday of any month at the county courthouse.

Your Rights as a Texas Homeowner

Texas offers some of the strongest homestead protections in the nation. If your property is your primary residence, you may qualify for a homestead exemption, which can reduce your property's taxable value. Additionally, Texas homestead laws protect your home from most creditors, though property tax liens are an important exception—the government can foreclose for unpaid taxes even on a homestead property.

Community Property Considerations

Texas is a community property state, which means that if you're married, both spouses typically share responsibility for property tax debt incurred during the marriage. This matters when selling a house with back taxes, as both spouses will generally need to be involved in the sale process and in resolving the tax liability.

Options for Selling Your House With Back Taxes

The good news is that having back taxes doesn't prevent you from selling your home. In fact, selling may be your best option to avoid foreclosure and protect your credit. Here are your main pathways forward:

1. Pay Off Back Taxes Before Selling

If you have savings or access to funds, paying off your tax debt before listing allows you to sell through traditional channels. However, with penalties and interest, the total amount owed may be significantly higher than the original tax bill. For many Dallas-Fort Worth homeowners facing financial hardship, coming up with this cash simply isn't feasible.

2. Sell With a Traditional Real Estate Agent

You can list your home with a Realtor even with tax liens attached. The back taxes will be paid from your proceeds at closing—title companies handle this routinely. However, this approach has drawbacks: listings can take 60-90 days or longer in the DFW market, you'll pay 5-6% in agent commissions, and you'll likely need to make repairs to attract buyers. If your tax foreclosure timeline is short, you may not have this kind of time.

How the Tax Payoff Works at Closing

When you sell a house with back taxes in Texas, the delinquent taxes become a lien on the property that must be satisfied before ownership can transfer. Here's how it typically works:

The title company conducting your closing will order a tax certificate from each taxing authority (county, city, school district, etc.) that shows the exact amount owed, including all penalties and interest through the closing date. This total is deducted from your sale proceeds and paid directly to the taxing authorities. You receive the remaining equity from the sale.

If you owe more in back taxes than your home's sale price, you have a more complex situation. Some taxing authorities may negotiate a settlement for less than the full amount owed, particularly if the alternative is a lengthy foreclosure process. Cash buyers experienced in these situations can often facilitate these negotiations.

Special Considerations in DFW Counties

Each county in the Dallas-Fort Worth area handles property tax collection slightly differently, though all follow Texas state law.

Dallas County

Dallas County typically sends multiple notices before pursuing foreclosure and maintains a list of tax-delinquent properties online. The county may refer your account to an attorney for collection if you don't respond to notices, which adds legal fees to your debt.

Tarrant County

Tarrant County, covering Fort Worth, Arlington, Grapevine, Colleyville, Southlake, and other western DFW cities, also provides notice before foreclosure. The county holds tax sales on the first Tuesday of each month at the Tarrant County Courthouse.

Collin County

Collin County, which includes Plano, Frisco, McKinney, Allen, and other northern suburbs, has seen rapidly increasing property values. This means your tax burden may have grown significantly even if the tax rate stayed the same. Many homeowners in Collin County are surprised by how much their taxes have increased due to home value appreciation.

Denton County

Denton County serves areas including Lewisville, Flower Mound, The Colony, Celina, and Prosper. Like other DFW counties, Denton County can pursue foreclosure on tax-delinquent properties and holds regular tax sales.

What Happens to Your Tax Debt After Selling?

Once your house sells and the back taxes are paid from the proceeds, your tax debt is completely resolved. The lien is released, the taxing authorities are satisfied, and you're free from that obligation. If there's equity remaining after paying off the taxes and any mortgages, that money goes to you.

This is vastly preferable to letting the property go to tax foreclosure, which damages your credit significantly and leaves you with no proceeds at all. Even if selling leaves you with little or no cash after paying debts, you've avoided foreclosure on your record and can begin rebuilding your financial situation.

HOA Dues and Other Liens

Many DFW properties, particularly in newer developments in Frisco, Prosper, Celina, and other growing areas, are part of homeowners associations. If you've fallen behind on property taxes, there's a good chance you may also owe HOA dues, which create a separate lien on your property.

HOA liens must also be satisfied at closing, just like tax liens. In Texas, HOAs have significant collection powers and can even foreclose on your home for unpaid dues. When you sell, the title company will contact your HOA for a payoff statement and settle those debts from your proceeds as well.

Time Is Your Most Valuable Asset

If you're behind on property taxes, the single most important thing to understand is that time matters. The longer you wait, the more you'll owe in penalties and interest, and the closer you get to a potential tax foreclosure. Taking action now—even if the situation feels difficult—gives you more options and more control over the outcome.

Selling your house before the tax foreclosure process advances protects your credit, may preserve some equity, and allows you to move forward on your own terms rather than the county's timeline.

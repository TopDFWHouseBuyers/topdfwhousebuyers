Texas Community Property Laws and Selling Your Home During Divorce

Published August 08, 2026 · Dallas-Fort Worth, Texas
Back to All Articles

Going through a divorce in Texas brings unique challenges, especially when it comes to selling your home. As one of only nine community property states, Texas has specific laws that govern how marital property—including your house—must be divided. Understanding these rules is essential for Dallas-Fort Worth homeowners who need to sell during or after a divorce.

If you're facing divorce in the Dallas-Fort Worth area, you're probably dealing with emotional stress, financial uncertainty, and difficult decisions about your shared home. The good news is that understanding Texas community property laws can help you navigate this challenging time with more confidence and clarity.

What Is Community Property in Texas?

Texas is a community property state, which means that most assets acquired during your marriage—including real estate—are considered equally owned by both spouses. This applies regardless of whose name is on the title or who made the mortgage payments.

Under Texas Family Code Section 3.002, community property includes almost everything either spouse earned or acquired during the marriage. Your home in Plano, Frisco, Allen, or anywhere else in the DFW area is likely community property if you purchased it while married, even if only one spouse's name appears on the deed.

Separate Property vs. Community Property

There are important exceptions to community property rules. Separate property in Texas includes:

  • Property owned before the marriage
  • Gifts or inheritances received by one spouse individually
  • Personal injury settlements (except for lost wages)
  • Property designated as separate in a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement

However, separate property can become community property through commingling. For example, if you owned a home in Richardson before marriage but used marital funds for mortgage payments or renovations, the property may have both separate and community components, requiring complex calculations during divorce.

How Texas Community Property Laws Affect Selling Your Home

When you decide to sell your marital home in the Dallas-Fort Worth area, both spouses typically must agree to the sale and sign all closing documents. This is true even if only one name is on the deed, because Texas community property laws give both spouses ownership rights.

The Requirement for Both Signatures

Texas law requires both spouses to sign documents when selling homestead property. The homestead exemption in Texas provides significant protections for primary residences, including creditor protections and property tax benefits. Because of these protections, even a non-titled spouse must consent to any sale or mortgage of the homestead property.

This means you cannot sell your Southlake, Colleyville, or Grapevine home without your spouse's signature, regardless of whose name appears on the title. Any attempt to do so could result in the sale being voided or challenged in court.

The Division of Sale Proceeds Under Texas Law

Texas follows a "just and right" division standard rather than a strict 50/50 split. While community property is presumed to be divided equally, judges have discretion to award a disproportionate share based on factors such as:

  • Each spouse's earning capacity and education
  • Fault in the breakup of the marriage
  • Benefits the innocent spouse would have received if the marriage continued
  • Disparity in earning power
  • Health and age of the parties
  • Size of separate estates
  • The needs of any children

When selling your home in McKinney, The Colony, or anywhere in DFW, the court will typically order how proceeds should be divided. If you sell before the divorce is finalized, the money is usually held in escrow or a trust account until the court determines the proper division.

Timing Your Home Sale During Divorce

Dallas-Fort Worth homeowners facing divorce have several options for when to sell their property, each with different implications.

Selling Before Filing for Divorce

Some couples choose to sell before officially filing. This can simplify the divorce process, but you must be careful to document how proceeds are divided. Without a court order, disputes can arise later. Both spouses should agree in writing about how to split the proceeds and where the money will be held.

Selling During Divorce Proceedings

If you sell while the divorce is pending, the court will typically issue orders about how to handle the proceeds. The sale may require court approval, and funds are usually placed in escrow until the final decree. This protects both parties and ensures fair distribution according to the final settlement.

Selling After the Divorce Is Final

Your divorce decree may award the home to one spouse with instructions to sell within a certain timeframe, or it may order an immediate sale with specific division instructions. Following the decree precisely is crucial to avoid contempt of court charges.

Property Taxes and Homestead Exemption

Texas offers generous homestead exemptions that reduce property tax burdens. When you sell, you'll lose this exemption. Additionally, both spouses should understand that while Texas has no state income tax, property taxes can be substantial, particularly in cities like Carrollton, Coppell, and Flower Mound. These ongoing costs affect the urgency of selling.

HOA Considerations

Many DFW neighborhoods, especially in master-planned communities throughout Plano, Frisco, and McKinney, have homeowners associations. Before selling, ensure all HOA dues are current. Unpaid assessments can complicate the sale and reduce your net proceeds. The divorce decree should specify who is responsible for HOA payments during any waiting period.

Protecting Your Rights During the Sale Process

When selling your marital home in Texas, take these steps to protect yourself:

Get Everything in Writing

Any agreement about selling your home should be documented in writing and, ideally, incorporated into a court order. Verbal agreements between divorcing spouses often lead to disputes.

Understand All Financial Implications

Calculate your net proceeds after paying off the mortgage, property taxes, HOA fees, closing costs, and any other liens. In Texas, certain creditors cannot place liens on homestead property, but this protection ends when you sell. Make sure you understand what debts will be paid from the proceeds.

Consider Tax Implications

The IRS allows up to $500,000 in capital gains exclusion for married couples selling their primary residence (or $250,000 for individuals). Timing your sale relative to your divorce finalization can affect which exclusion applies. Consult a tax professional about your specific situation.

Work with Experienced Professionals

Divorce involving real estate requires expertise in both family law and real estate transactions. Work with attorneys, tax advisors, and real estate professionals who understand Texas community property laws and the DFW market.

If your spouse refuses to agree to a sale or won't sign documents, you may need to petition the court for an order compelling the sale. Texas courts have authority to order the sale of community property and can even appoint a receiver to sign documents on behalf of an uncooperative spouse.

When You Can't Agree on Price

Disagreements about listing price or whether to accept an offer are common. Consider hiring a neutral appraiser to determine fair market value, or let the court decide. With a cash buyer, you receive a straightforward offer that removes pricing negotiations from the equation.

When the Home Needs Repairs

When Equity Is Underwater or Minimal
